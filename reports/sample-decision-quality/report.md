# Executive Summary

Most organizations judge their decisions by outcomes. A product that sells well was a good bet; an acquisition that disappoints was a bad one. This instinct is understandable and deeply misleading. Outcomes mix the quality of a decision with luck, timing and the actions of others, and a single result says little about which of these dominated. The research literature on [judgment under uncertainty](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124) offers a better standard: a good decision is one made through a sound process, with the information reasonably available, and with an honest view of the odds. That standard can be taught, measured and improved, and it is the subject of this report.

The case for attention is growing. Decision cycles are shortening, the volume of information that decision-makers must filter is rising, and AI systems now draft analyses, score options and generate forecasts inside everyday workflows. These tools can raise the floor of decision quality by reducing [noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise:_A_Flaw_in_Human_Judgment) and surfacing evidence that people would otherwise miss. They can also lower the ceiling, by encouraging [automation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation_bias), hiding uncertainty behind fluent prose, and concentrating error when many teams rely on the same model. Which of these effects dominates depends far less on the technology than on the decision process that surrounds it.

::: keyfindings
1. **Heuristics are not the enemy.** Simple rules of thumb often match or beat complex models in unstable, data-poor environments. Failures arise when a heuristic is used outside the environment it fits, not because heuristics exist.
2. **Process predicts outcomes better than talent.** Across the evidence we review, structured practices such as reference-class forecasting, pre-mortems and independent estimates improve accuracy more reliably than hiring for individual brilliance.
3. **Calibration is trainable.** Forecasting tournaments show that short training, teaming and frequent feedback measurably improve probabilistic accuracy, and that the gains persist.
4. **AI shifts where errors occur.** Machine assistance reduces random inconsistency but introduces correlated error and over-reliance. Teams that keep human judgment active get the benefit without the fragility.
5. **What gets measured gets better.** Organizations that log decisions, state probabilities and score them later build a feedback loop that outcome-based reviews cannot provide.
:::

Our review draws three practical conclusions. First, leaders should separate the evaluation of decisions from the evaluation of outcomes, and should review a sample of decisions on process grounds regardless of how they turned out. Second, the highest-return interventions are cheap: writing down a forecast with an explicit probability, asking "what would have to be true?" before a commitment, and gathering independent judgments before discussion. Third, AI adoption in decision-making should be governed as a change to the decision process, not as a software procurement, with clear rules on when the model advises, when it decides and when it must be overruled.

::: stats
- 5 | practices that account for most of the improvement we observe in decision audits
- 3 | failure modes that AI introduces: over-reliance, correlated error and false precision

:::

## Who this report is for

This report is written for executives, board members and the people who support them: strategy and planning teams, risk and finance functions, and the growing number of leaders responsible for deploying AI inside their organizations. It assumes no background in psychology or statistics. Where technical ideas such as calibration or proper scoring rules matter to the argument, they are explained in plain terms and linked to accessible sources for readers who want more depth.

The evidence base is broad but uneven. Some findings, such as the planning fallacy, the benefits of aggregating independent estimates and the trainability of probabilistic judgment, rest on decades of replicated research across many settings. Others, particularly those concerning human–AI collaboration with modern language models, come from a young literature in which results are still accumulating and sometimes conflict. We indicate the strength of evidence throughout, and we distinguish between what the research shows and what we infer from it.

Finally, a note on ambition. The practices recommended here will not eliminate bad outcomes; no process can. Their aim is to shift the odds, decision by decision, so that an organization's portfolio of choices performs better over time. Small, consistent improvements in the quality of many decisions compound into a durable advantage, which is why we argue that decision quality deserves the same management attention as operational efficiency or financial discipline.

The remainder of the report is organized as follows. @sec:foundations reviews the science of heuristics and biases and the more recent ecological view of rationality. @sec:failures maps where organizational decisions fail in practice. @sec:forecasting turns to the evidence from forecasting research and what it implies for calibration. @sec:ai examines AI as a decision partner, @sec:operating-model sets out an operating model, @sec:measurement describes how to measure decision quality, and @sec:outlook closes with an outlook and a set of recommendations.

The recommendations are deliberately modest in cost. None requires a new platform or a large consulting engagement; most require a small number of people to change how they write down and review the choices they make. The organizations that do this consistently tend to discover that their decision quality was never limited by information or intelligence, but by the absence of a feedback loop. Building that loop is the central task of decision leadership in the coming decade, and AI makes it both more urgent and more achievable.
