# AI as a Decision Partner {#ai}

## An old debate in a new form

The question of whether machines should assist or replace human judgment is not new. In 1954 the psychologist Paul Meehl published *Clinical versus Statistical Prediction*, comparing the accuracy of clinicians' judgments with simple statistical formulas across a range of prediction tasks. The formulas matched or beat the clinicians in nearly every comparison. A later meta-analysis by [Grove and colleagues (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1037/1040-3590.12.1.19), covering 136 studies, reached a similar conclusion: mechanical prediction was about as accurate or more accurate than clinical prediction in the large majority of cases.

Despite this evidence, adoption of statistical decision aids has been slow. One reason is [algorithm aversion](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000033): research by Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons and Cade Massey found that people lose confidence in an algorithm more quickly than in a human forecaster after seeing both make the same mistake. People hold algorithms to a standard of perfection they do not apply to themselves or to other people. Interestingly, the same researchers found that aversion falls sharply when people are allowed to adjust the algorithm's output, even slightly.

Large language models and other modern AI systems change this debate in two ways. They extend machine assistance from narrow, structured prediction tasks to the open-ended, language-based work that makes up much of professional decision-making: framing problems, summarizing evidence, generating options and drafting recommendations. And they present their output in fluent natural language, which makes them feel more like colleagues than formulas. Both changes raise the potential value of machine assistance and the risk that it is used badly.

## Where machine assistance helps

The evidence and early field experience point to four areas where AI tends to improve decision quality.

**Reducing noise.** Because a model returns consistent outputs for consistent inputs, it removes much of the unwanted variability that noise audits reveal in human judgment. Even when a model is no more accurate on average than a skilled professional, combining the two often reduces total error by damping the human's inconsistency.

**Widening the search.** Models are good at generating alternatives, counter-arguments and considerations that a decision team has missed. Used as a structured devil's advocate, they can counteract narrow framing and confirmatory search, two of the most common failures described in @sec:failures.

**Imposing the outside view.** Models trained on large datasets can supply base rates and reference-class information quickly, making it easier to start an estimate from the outside view.

**Documenting reasoning.** AI tools lower the cost of writing a decision record, summarizing the evidence considered and the reasoning applied. This makes later review, and learning, much more practical.

**Automation bias and complacency.** Decades of research in aviation, medicine and process control, reviewed by [Parasuraman and Manzey (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055), show that people working with automated aids tend to over-rely on them: they accept incorrect recommendations (errors of commission) and fail to notice problems the aid did not flag (errors of omission). This [automation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation_bias) affects experts as well as novices and is not reliably cured by training alone. It grows with the aid's general reliability, which creates a paradox: the better the system usually is, the less likely people are to catch its occasional failures.

**Correlated error.** When many analysts consult the same model, their judgments become correlated. The wisdom-of-crowds effect depends on errors being independent; a shared model removes that independence. An organization that once had twenty analysts making partly independent errors may now have twenty analysts making the same error. The risk extends across organizations when many firms rely on the same few foundation models.

**False precision and fluent confabulation.** Language models produce confident, well-structured prose regardless of the strength of the underlying evidence. They may cite sources that do not exist, present estimates without meaningful uncertainty, or reconcile conflicting evidence with a plausible but unfounded narrative. Fluency is a powerful cue to credibility in human cognition, so these errors are harder to detect than the obviously wrong outputs of earlier systems.

## Evidence on human–AI teams

Research on human–AI collaboration is growing quickly, and its findings are more nuanced than either enthusiasts or sceptics suggest. A consistent pattern is that human–AI teams outperform humans alone on tasks within the model's competence, but can underperform the model alone when humans override it in the wrong cases. On tasks at or beyond the edge of the model's competence, teams frequently do worse than humans alone, because people accept confident but incorrect AI suggestions.

The practical lesson is that the value of AI assistance depends heavily on whether users know where the model's competence ends. That boundary is often jagged and invisible: a model may excel at one task and fail at a superficially similar one. Organizations therefore need explicit guidance on which decisions AI may inform, which it may make, and which require independent human judgment, along with routine checks that the boundary is where they think it is.

## Governance frameworks

Regulators and standards bodies have begun to codify expectations for AI in consequential decisions. The [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), released in January 2023, organizes AI risk management into four functions, *Govern*, *Map*, *Measure* and *Manage*, and emphasizes that trustworthy AI depends on the context of use. The European Union's [AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/) takes a risk-based approach, imposing obligations on high-risk systems, including requirements for human oversight designed to prevent automation bias. The [OECD AI Principles](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles) set out high-level commitments to transparency, robustness and accountability that many national frameworks reference.

| Framework | Scope | Treatment of human oversight | Relevance to decision process |

These frameworks share an important premise with the decision-quality literature: the risks of AI arise not from the model alone but from the interaction between model, user and decision process. Governance that focuses only on model accuracy will miss the most common failure, which is a sound model used in an unsound process.
